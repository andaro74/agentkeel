# M10 — Outsiders come in by the front door

## In one sentence

Someone outside the team can only ask an agent a question through the
platform's front door, under a name the agent's owners agreed to; every
answer still carries the agent's rules, every question is recorded with
who asked, and a stranger is turned away and the attempt written down.

**Read this sentence with what is and is not built, from the day it was
written** (SPEC/00 §10.5). At open there is no front door: an agent is
reached only by the platform itself, and no record of any question says who
asked it. M10 is meant to build the door and the record, and to show the
door turning a stranger away on a case we planted for it. Until that has
been read, nothing on this page says the door works.

## Why it matters

Every agent this platform has deployed has answered exactly one caller: the
platform. Its own pipeline asks each agent its test questions after a
deploy, and the owner has called one by hand. Nobody outside the platform's
cloud account can reach an agent at all, which is safe and useless: an
agent nobody can ask is not a product. The moment a team wants its agent to
answer a partner, a studio desk or an app, three questions arrive at once.
Who is allowed to ask? Does the agent's own rulebook still apply when the
question comes from outside? And afterwards, can anyone say who asked what?

That is the outside-caller threat in SPEC/00 §3: someone reaching an agent
around the door the platform meant everyone to use, or under a name the
agent's owners never agreed to, or leaving no trace of having asked. The fix
has to do three things at once and let none of them slip: one way in, a
list of who may use it that a named owner controls, and a written record of
every attempt, kept where the agent itself cannot touch it. M10 is meant to
build that and to check it the only way that counts: by trying to get in
the wrong way and reading whether the attempt was stopped and written down.
At open none of that exists; "What happened" says what the close read.

## What we planted

We took the festival desk, the fictional agent M09 made, which is deployed
and answering today, and asked what its record of a question looks like. It
names the agent, the commit, the model, the rules on the call and the
answer, and it names no one who asked. We kept a copy of that record as it
is, and wrote beside it the records we expect the door to leave: a known
caller let in, a stranger turned away, someone with no name at all turned
away, and someone trying to walk straight to the agent around the door,
turned away too. We also wrote the records a door must never leave: a
stranger let in, an attempt that got round the door, a record naming the
wrong person, and a refusal written down too late.

We looked at the agent's settings file. Nothing in it says who may call the
agent, and the platform's check does not ask. We kept a copy of that file,
and beside it the same file with one line added naming one allowed caller,
which the platform's own checks reject today because the line is unknown to
them. We also wrote the change a team might make to let one more caller in
with a single owner's signature, which the two-signature rule passes today
because it has never heard of callers.

Then we planted the readers' absence. Nothing in the platform reads a
caller's record; nothing refuses a settings file that names no callers;
nothing asks for a second signature when a caller is added. Each of those
is a test that fails today for exactly that reason, committed before any
code that could make it pass. Two guards were planted too, and they pass:
the agent's rules sit on its model call, where a door in front cannot
reach them, and the reference agent's seven planted questions are still
stopped. The door must leave both alone.

## What happened

It worked, and the row is **GREEN**. The numbers in the next three
paragraphs are the envelope's (`14ab792…`), read by the platform's own
observer from the call records in the security account, from the agent's
answer record, and from the festival desk's pull request. The numbers in
the last paragraph are the accounts' and GitHub's, read by hand into the
milestone's close.

**The door let one caller in and turned three away.** The festival
desk's team named one allowed caller in the agent's settings file, in a
pull request the platform's check admitted. The platform put a door in
front of the agent and set the agent to answer the door and nothing
else. Then four calls were made. The named caller asked a question and
was answered: **1 of 1 admitted**, the record naming the caller. A call
with no name, a call under a name from somewhere else, and a call that
went straight to the agent around the door were each turned away: **3 of
3 refused**. All four left a record in the security account, each saying
who asked, or that no name was given, including the stranger's borrowed
name and the role that tried to go around. The records arrived 96, 78,
107 and 235 seconds after those four calls, in that order, against a
limit of 600 seconds.

**The agent's rules stayed on.** When the platform put the door on, it
asked the agent its three test questions through the door, as the
deploy's log records. The record of that names the same rulebook as
before, and the planted forbidden question was stopped: 1 of 1. The
reference agent, which has no door, kept all seven of its planted
questions stopped.

**What GREEN does not mean.** GREEN means one named caller was let in
once, three wrong callers were turned away once each, and every attempt
was written down in time, on one agent on one day. It does not mean the
record cannot be forged: anything in the platform's own cloud account
that can send log lines could file a line under an agent's name, because
the security account's inbox can only be told which account may send,
not which role, and the platform shares its account. The door's own
records are told from a forgery by the request numbers the owner wrote
down, and the close names the ways to fix that. It does not mean a
second caller needs two signatures in the team's repository: there,
adding a name takes one commit; only the platform's own repository asks
for two. It does not mean a call with no name made straight at the agent
is recorded: the door records it, the agent itself does not, and nothing
watches for the record stream being switched off. It does not mean a
stranger can be rate limited or billed: the door admits or refuses and
counts nothing. And the "outside caller" was a name the platform itself
issued, held by the one person who holds every seat. It does not mean
the door's own list is locked: a change to the platform's door code
could admit another issuer under one review, and no two-signature rule
reads that change. And the door is on one agent; the reference agent has
none.

**What it cost, in plain numbers.** Four pull requests, the cap. The
platform's own deploy path was refused three times and silently failed
once before the door stood with its records arriving, each a repair: a
permission document over its size limit, two missing permissions, six
the service needed to write the door's own log, and an inbox that would
not accept the platform's mail. The first door deploy's failure repeated
on a timer until its repair landed, and each repeat left one abandoned
sign-in pool: 55 in all, deleted by hand. The live run moved from the
third pull request to the fourth. Seven account changes were made before
the ruling that carries them was signed, the last two refused by the
service, and each is recorded as such. The narrowing Security asked for
after the third pull request's review cannot be deployed at all, and
stands as a named shortcoming. Each is written down with an owner in the
milestone's close (`milestones/M10/README.md`).

## For the business user

What M10 is meant to show: a team can open its agent to a named outside
caller without opening it to everyone, the agent's own rules stay on every
answer, and every question leaves a record of who asked, including the ones
that were turned away. The agent's Tool Owner writes the list of who may
call; Security decides what the door is; the Threshold Owner's ten-minute
bar for a refusal reaching the record is proposed to be reused from M05,
which that seat rules before PR 2. One person holds
every one of those seats in this project.

Not measured at M10, stated now. One agent gets a door; the reference agent
does not, and nothing stops a team's own repository from adding a caller
with one signature, because the two-signature rule only runs on the
platform's own repository. Whether a refused attempt at the service's edge
leaves a record at all is not known at open; it is the first thing checked
before the door is built, and if it cannot be recorded in time this
milestone closes RED on that point. No rate limit, no budget per caller and
no gateway are built. The caller is an identity the platform issues, not a
second person: one human still holds every seat.
