# M09 — Every agent gets its own rules

## In one sentence

A team writes the rules for its own agent on top of the platform's, each
rule is shown to stop the question it was written for, and a team can add
rules but cannot take the platform's away.

**Read this sentence with what is and is not measured, from the day it was
written** (SPEC/00 §10.5). "The platform's" rules on day one are two: a
phone number and an email address are hidden in any answer. Neither has
ever been shown to fire on a question planted for it in this project, and
M09 does not plant one; what M09 shows firing is the team's own rule. That
the platform's rules are in the agent's guardrail is read from the guardrail
itself, not from a question.

## Why it matters

Until now every agent made from the template was guarded by the reference
agent's rules, which are about one subject: whether a film can be shown
where and when. A team that made an agent for something else got those
rules and nothing of its own, and a `rules/` folder in its repository was
not read. Worse, nothing could show that any rule stopped anything on that
team's agent, because its repository could not hold the kind of test that
asks a forbidden question.

That is the negligent-developer threat in its quietest form: an agent on a
new subject shipped with someone else's rules and nobody the wiser. The fix
has two halves, and they pull against each other. A team must be able to
write rules for its own subject, and the platform must keep a floor under
every agent that no team can lower. M09 is meant to build both, and to
check them the only way that counts: by planting a question each rule is
meant to stop and reading whether it did. At open none of that existed; "What happened" says what the close read.

## What we planted

We made a fictional agent from the template and called it the festival
desk. It is the reference agent's own code and data, answering the same
question about where a title may be published, with one rule added that
the reference agent does not have, written the same way the reference
agent's rules are: do not reveal which titles a festival has selected
before the announcement. We planted one test question that rule exists to
stop, and a line saying which rule must stop it. We pointed its settings at
a guardrail of its own instead of the reference agent's. The reference
agent's six film rules are not carried over: under its own guardrail the
festival desk is guarded by the platform's floor and its one rule, and
nothing else, which is the point and also the risk the close must read.

Then we handed it to the platform's check, as a team's first pull request
would. The check refused it, twice: the guardrail is not the reference
agent's, and an agent's tests may only be ordinary questions and trick
questions, never a forbidden one. That refusal is the planted failure. We
also planted a rules file that tries to switch off one of the platform's
two rules, and a record of a run where the planted question was stopped by
the wrong rule, or not stopped at all, so the readers that arrive next must
catch each of those too.

## What happened

It worked, and the row is **GREEN**. The numbers in the next three
paragraphs are the envelope's (`c503324…`), read by the platform's own
observer from the agent's answer record, from the guardrail it was built
with, and from its three pull requests. The numbers in the last paragraph
are the record's, GitHub's and the account's, read by hand into the
milestone's close.

**The team's rule fired.** The festival desk was made into a real agent
repository from the template, as a team would, with its one rule and its
one planted question. The platform's check read the rule, accepted the
agent's own guardrail in place of the reference agent's, and accepted the
forbidden question as a test. The platform built the guardrail from the
platform's floor plus that one rule, deployed the agent, and asked it its
three questions. The planted question was stopped, and the trace names
the team's rule as the one that stopped it: **1 of 1**. The ordinary question and the trap were answered, each with its row and
clause, as the deploy's log records (run 38342463397); the envelope reads
the plant only. The new rule did not get in their way.

**The floor held.** The guardrail on the agent's call was read back from
the account: it carries the platform's two rules (phone and email hidden)
and the team's one, and its label carries the digest of exactly those rule
files, computed on its own by the observer. A second pull request that
tried to switch off the email rule was refused by the platform's check and
never merged; the pull request's own record names the email rule and the
change. The reference agent
kept all seven of its own planted questions stopped after the floor was
split out of its rules.

**What GREEN does not mean.** GREEN means one team rule fired once on one
planted question, and the floor was present and could not be lowered. It
does not mean the floor itself has been shown to fire: the platform's two
rules have never stopped a planted question in this project, and this
milestone did not plant one for them. It does not mean a silent rule
stops a team's change: at M09 a rule that fails to fire is recorded after
the merge, not refused before it. It does not mean the wording of a rule
will work first time: this one did, once.

**What it cost, in plain numbers.** Four pull requests, the cap. Two
repairs to the platform's own deploy path before an agent with its own
rules could be deployed at all, so the live run moved from the third pull
request to the fourth. Fifty failed deploy attempts stand in the record
between the first refusal and the repair's merge, on a ten-minute schedule,
harmless. Thirty minutes from the team's merge to the first deploy attempt
and nineteen from the repair's merge to the deploy that landed, because
the check and the deploy each run on a timer. One new repository had to
be added to the platform's GitHub App by hand before its rules could be
applied, which the platform's own specification said would not be needed.
One account change was made on a draft ruling that was signed afterwards.
Two older test fixtures had to be moved because they had hard-coded the
platform's rule version. Each is written down with an owner in the
milestone's close (`milestones/M09/README.md`).

## For the business user

What M09 is meant to show: a team can give its own agent its own rules and
see each one work, while the platform's floor stays under every agent and
no team can lower it. The team's Rule Owner writes the agent's rules; the
platform's Rule Owner writes the floor; Security decides who is allowed to
build the guardrail. One person holds every one of those seats in this
project.

Not measured at M09, stated now. The platform's floor on day one hides
phone numbers and email addresses, and that floor has never been shown
firing on a planted question here; M09 reads that it is present, not that
it fires. A silent rule in a team's repository does not stop that team's
change from going in at M09; it is recorded after the fact and read into
this row. Whether the rule's wording stops the planted question on the
first try is not known: the reference agent needed six attempts at one of
its rules. If it does not, the rule is reworded, never the question. The
gateway and policy engine some readers may expect are not built, and a
caller for the public is proposed as the next milestone, not this one.
